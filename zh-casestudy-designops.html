Context

Process isn't the opposite of creativity — it's what lets it scale. When I joined, the team had talent but not the infrastructure to apply it consistently or sustainably.

The operational gaps were predictable: work starting before requirements were clear, no shared understanding of what "done" looked like at each stage, research findings that went undiscovered, and a design system that was hard to contribute to. None of these were the team's fault — they were process failures, and they were mine to fix.

Design Workflow

A five-phase workflow that the whole product org could follow

I introduced a structured phase model to make design progress visible across teams and give designers a clear sense of what was expected at each point in a project.

Phase 01
Discovery
Problem definition, user research, context gathering before design starts.
Phase 02
Pre-Production
Brief finalised. Deadlines, polish level and success criteria agreed.
Phase 03
Building
Active design — explorations, iterations, critique and sign-off.
Phase 04
Shipping
Handoff to engineering, QA, and release coordination.
Phase 05
DIMP
Design-led improvements to released work — quality, accessibility, system.

The fifth phase — DIMP (Design-led Improvement) — was deliberately separate from the feature roadmap. It gave designers protected time after shipping to improve what had already been released: tightening accessibility, updating Flapjack components, addressing visual inconsistencies that had been deferred under time pressure. Three days per designer per month were allocated to DIMP work.

I had to define clearly what DIMP was and wasn't. It wasn't a UX bug tracker, a support backlog, or a vehicle for new features. It was a mechanism for design-led quality, and treating it as such protected it from being colonised by other teams' priorities.

Requirements

A design brief template that clarified before work started

A significant amount of rework traced back to misaligned expectations at the start of a project. The brief template was a direct intervention on that.

"Designers shouldn't have to infer what 'done' looks like. If expectations around deadline, polish level and success criteria aren't explicit before work starts, they'll be contested when work ends."

What it captured
The problem being solved, project requirements, delivery expectations, deadline, required level of polish (exploratory sketch vs final UI), measures of success, dependencies and key stakeholders — all agreed before design began.
Why it mattered
The template gave designers something to push back with. If a PM wanted a fully polished UI in two days for an exploratory brief, the form made that tension visible — and created a constructive way to negotiate scope before effort was invested.

Design System

Raising Flapjack's maturity — and the team's relationship to it

Flapjack was the internal name for zeroheight's own design system. As the company that sells design system tooling to others, the bar for internal maturity was unusually high.

When I joined, contribution to Flapjack was informal and inconsistently applied. Designers weren't sure how to propose changes, engineers weren't sure when to expect design decisions, and the reporting on system adoption was minimal. I approached this as an operations problem as much as a craft problem.

There was also an obvious obligation — and opportunity — that came with the context. We were a company selling design system tooling to teams who wanted exactly what we were trying to build internally. So we used zeroheight itself as the single source of truth for Flapjack: documenting components, tracking their status, publishing release notes, and making everything accessible to the whole product team in one place. Eating our own dog food wasn't just a principle — it was the most credible signal we could send to customers, and a genuine test of whether the product held up under real-world pressure.

Contribution System
How changes enter the system
Documented how contributions should be proposed, reviewed, designed, built and released — creating a clear path from "this doesn't feel right" to "this is now the standard", without all decisions needing to route through a single gatekeeper.
Collaboration Model
Three stages: Creating, Updating, Reporting
Identified the three design-engineering collaboration points that most often produced friction or silence, and designed a working model around each: clearer handoffs when creating new components, a structured update process, and regular reporting on system health and adoption.
Token & Component Work
Q2 2025 objectives
Led a focused quarter of system improvements: colour tokens, typography tokens, iconography, visual assets within the product, modal patterns, and error UI patterns — each with a named owner and a defined scope.
Figma Governance
Library permissions and organisation
Clarified Figma library structure, permissions and team access — reducing friction for designers working across product areas and making it easier for new starters to navigate the system.
Dogfooding
Using zeroheight to run zeroheight's design system
We documented Flapjack inside zeroheight itself — component status, usage guidance, release notes, contribution rules — and made it available to the entire product team. Running our own system through our own product was both a credibility test and a feedback loop that directly informed how we understood and improved the platform.

Culture & Critique

Making critique useful, and design culture something to invest in

Critique sessions that feel like performance reviews don't produce better work. I wanted to build a team culture where feedback was expected, welcomed, and used.

Design critique
More accessible, more structured
I introduced a more structured critique format — with clear framing, defined feedback types, and a rotating facilitation model that took the pressure off any single person. The aim was to make critique a regular part of the team's rhythm rather than something that only happened when work felt uncertain.
DIMP days
Monthly design-improvement themes
Beyond the per-sprint DIMP allocation, I introduced a monthly theme — a focus area for design-led quality that the whole team worked on together. This might be an accessibility pass on a specific area of the product, a Flapjack update, or a consistency audit. It gave DIMP days a shared direction rather than leaving each designer to define their own scope.
Research
A research hub the team would actually use
Research findings were scattered — useful insights existed but were hard to find and rarely built on. I introduced a centralised research hub and pushed for regular user testing as a team practice rather than a project-specific activity, including a structured UserTesting benchmark programme.
Workshops
Design-led sessions that built cross-functional relationships
I ran and facilitated workshops including a Figma library pain-points session with engineering, a Flapjack standards survey, and a Business Value of Design session with the team. These were as much about building shared vocabulary and trust as they were about the specific output.
Reflection

What I'd tell someone inheriting this kind of role

DesignOps work is invisible when it's working well — which makes it easy to underinvest in until something breaks. The brief template, the phase model, the DIMP allocation: none of these felt dramatic when I introduced them, but they removed a consistent source of friction that had been draining the team's energy and credibility.

The hardest part of building operational standards isn't designing them — it's creating the conditions where people follow them by choice rather than compliance. That required explaining the why, giving the team ownership of specific areas, and being willing to revise things that weren't working in practice. The contribution system for Flapjack went through several iterations before it stuck.

If I had one regret, it's that I didn't move the research infrastructure forward sooner. The intent was always there, but it got deprioritised in favour of more immediate deliverables. By the time I left, we had a solid foundation — but earlier investment would have made the team's case studies significantly stronger.
