Context

A growing product team without operational foundations

Aircall is a cloud-based communications platform serving sales and support teams worldwide. By 2022, the product design organisation had grown across multiple experience squads — but without a shared operating model to match.

Designers across squads were making independent decisions about file structure, handoff, tooling, and process — leading to inconsistencies that slowed collaboration and made onboarding new team members harder than it needed to be. The design system (Tractor) existed but lacked the documentation and contribution infrastructure to be used reliably across teams.

In March 2022 I led the creation of a formal DesignOps programme — framed as a presentation to the team, with a roadmap across four quarters, and followed four months later by an Epilogue documenting what had been delivered. I also wrote the Figma Playbook: a comprehensive guide to how Aircall's product design team used Figma across every level of the tooling stack.

Framework

Defining DesignOps for the team — and making the case for it

Before building anything, I needed to establish a shared understanding of what DesignOps was and why it mattered. The team were designers, not ops practitioners — the framing had to be practical and grounded in their day-to-day frustrations.

"DesignOps refers to the orchestration and optimization of people, processes, and craft in order to amplify design's value and impact at scale." — Nielsen Norman Group

I structured the programme around the idea of working on the 'how' — not the design work itself, but the conditions under which that work happened. The seven pillars spanned three categories: things the team needed to do together (Together), things that enabled individual designers to work well (Done), and things that would create lasting impact on the business and product quality (Impact).

Design System — Tractor (ongoing)

The roadmap made it clear that this wasn't a single project — it was a sustained programme. Design rituals came first because they required the least infrastructure to change and would immediately improve team culture. The design system ran as a continuous thread throughout, given its foundational nature. Onboarding and handoff were deferred to Q3–Q4 to give earlier pillars time to bed in first.

Figma Playbook

A 24-slide governance guide for every designer and collaborator at Aircall

The Figma Playbook was written to give anyone touching Aircall's design files — from junior designers to PMs and developers — a clear, consistent understanding of how the tool was structured and how to navigate it responsibly.

The playbook covered the full stack: from role-based permissions (Owner, Editor, Viewer, Guest) and the rationale behind access control, through to file creation standards, page naming conventions, branch and review workflows, prototyping practices, and developer inspect guidance for handoff.

The experience team structure — Agent, Admin, Integrations, Aircallee, Reseller, Brand, Marketing, DesignOps — was mapped directly into Figma's team and project hierarchy, so the tooling reflected how the organisation actually worked.

Access & Permissions
Four roles, clearly defined
Owner/Admin, Editor, Viewer, and Guest — each with explicit permissions for viewing, commenting, editing, and library access. Designers held full editor access; collaborators like PMs and developers were set as Viewers. Reduced accidental overwrites and protected work in progress.
File Architecture
Teams → Projects → Files → Pages
A four-level hierarchy matching the team structure. Each experience team had its own Figma team space with dedicated projects per product area. Files were broken down by platform (Desktop, iOS, Android) and each contained a cover page for quick identification.
Branches & Reviews
Design version control
Branches allowed designers to explore changes without touching source files. Naming conventions were defined, review workflows documented, and the merge-or-archive decision clarified — following development version control logic to reduce file bloat and protect the source of truth.
Handoff & Inspect
Developer-ready delivery standards
Developers could access spacing, padding and measurements via the Inspect panel. Prototype flows were named and described to assist external reviewers — PMs, developers, and user testers — with clear signposting at every stage of the handoff.
Reflection

What this work produced beyond the deliverables

DesignOps work at this scale — building from scratch within an existing organisation — requires a particular kind of political intelligence alongside the craft. You're introducing structure that some people will experience as constraint. The framing matters: this wasn't presented as a set of rules, but as infrastructure the team had asked for, built to make their own work less frustrating.

The Epilogue was as important as the original presentation. Returning four months later with a transparent account of what had been built, what was still in progress, and what had changed in scope demonstrated that this was a real programme with accountability — not a slide deck that got filed away. It also gave the team a moment to see how much had actually moved in a short time.

The Figma Playbook outlasted my tenure at Aircall as a living reference document. Writing it forced a level of clarity about tooling decisions that was useful for the team beyond its immediate purpose — it became part of the design onboarding kit that the DesignOps programme was itself trying to build.
